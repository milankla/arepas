# Berkeley Survey Point Data — Summary Report

**Date:** June 24, 2026
**Source:** `Berkeley_SURVEYPOINT` (provided by the City, exported June 2026)
**Neighborhood:** Berkeley, Denver, Colorado

## What this data is

This is the **list of buildings to survey** in the Berkeley neighborhood — the
starting point for our work there. It contains **3,527 buildings**, each placed
at its real-world map location, along with basic information about the property.

Importantly, this is **not** finished survey data and it does **not** include any   
photographs yet. Per the City's guidance, field photography for Berkeley begins
in **early July**. This file simply tells the program **which buildings exist and
where they are**, so we know what to go out and capture. No buildings are filtered
out — all 3,527 are included regardless of age or condition.

## What information each building comes with

Almost every building arrives with a strong set of basic facts:

| Information | How complete | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Street address | 100% | 3,511 unique addresses (a few shared by multi-unit properties) |
| City, location/parcel descriptors | 100% | Township, range, section, legal description |
| Map coordinates | 100% | Precise location for every building |
| Street number, name, direction | ~100% | |
| Year built | 97.6% | Mostly early 1900s–1920s — Berkeley is a historic area |
| Subdivision / plat year | ~97% | When the area was originally platted |

So for locating and organizing the survey, the data is essentially complete.

## The key gap: Smithsonian numbers

A **Smithsonian number** is the official statewide identifier assigned to a
historic resource once it has been formally recorded. It is the standard "ID card"
that ties a building to the official historic-resource record.

In this dataset, **only 94 of the 3,527 buildings (about 2.7%) have a Smithsonian
number.** The other **roughly 97% do not have one yet.**

### Why this matters

- **Most Berkeley buildings have never been formally recorded.** A missing
  Smithsonian number generally means the building has not yet been through an
  official historic survey. That is expected here — Berkeley is a *new* survey
  area for us, and assigning these identifiers is part of what the broader
  survey effort produces.
- **We cannot rely on the Smithsonian number as each building's identifier.**
  Because it is present for only a small fraction of properties, it can't be the
  primary way we label and track buildings through our process. We will need a
  consistent identifier that exists for **every** building (the street address is
  the natural candidate, since it is complete and nearly always unique).
- **It confirms the nature of the task.** These are buildings to be *newly*
  documented, not previously cataloged records we are simply updating. Our work —
  capturing images and classifying each building — is part of building that
  official record for the first time.

A related note: a few other "official record" fields (assessment details and
recording organization) are filled in for only the same small group of ~2–3% of
buildings — the handful that have already been touched by prior survey work. This
reinforces the picture: **the vast majority of Berkeley is undocumented and
awaiting survey.**

## Bottom line

The Berkeley data gives us a clean, complete map of **what to survey and where** —
3,527 buildings with reliable addresses, locations, and build dates. The one thing
it largely lacks is the official **Smithsonian historic-resource number**, present
for fewer than 3% of buildings. That absence is not a data problem to fix; it is a
reflection of reality: **most of Berkeley has not yet been formally recorded, and
documenting it is exactly what this project is here to help do.**
